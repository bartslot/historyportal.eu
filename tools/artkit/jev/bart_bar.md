# How Bart judges History Portal work (the house bar)

History Portal makes history lessons for pupils aged about 8-18 in European classrooms (Italian, Dutch,
German, English). Lessons are illustrated stories with narration, speaking characters and quizzes.
The aim: entertaining AND historically correct, like a real play. There is room to play.

## Truth and history
- Only facts that the lesson's sources support. If unsure, leave it out; never invent.
- Invented dialogue, jokes, emotions and inner thoughts are fine. Invented events, wrong dates, people
  in places where they were not, and a legend told as fact are not.
- Three planes stay separate and visible: real history, the world inside a literary work (e.g. the
  Divine Comedy), and later tradition or legend ("people told that...").
- A work of literature is evidence of what the work says, not of what happened.
- An anachronism is something that DATES the picture wrongly, not something modern in origin. Clouds,
  sea and terrain are fine in any era; city lights, later buildings, later furniture forms, glass
  windows in a 13th-century house, a 19th-century lamp, later facades or domes are not.

## Story and script
- Characters carry the story: events, arguments, feelings and facts. The narrator only frames (time,
  place, what happens off-stage). A narrator repeating what a character says, or a character echoing
  the narrator, is "lame".
- Every scene needs a want, an obstacle and a turn. Characters need personality and faces with
  emotion. Humour is welcome where the moment allows; grave moments stay grave.
- Many panels per scene, like a comic: wide shots, over-the-shoulder, inserts, close-ups of faces.
  Too few images and children get bored.

## Pictures (the history-line style)
- One style: black ink lines on pure white paper, restrained comic shading. About 10-14% black,
  65-75% white, single-direction hatching, cross-hatching at most 20% of the shade.
- Never: solid black fills or silhouettes, dense engraved/copperplate cross-hatching, grey washes,
  dark muddy images, AI clutter, fused objects, blur. Characters must be in focus and sharp.
- Composition must make physical sense: a person writing sits on the room's own chair at the desk and
  writes on the desk. Two chairs for one writer, writing on the knees, floating figures, figures
  randomly placed or at the wrong size are rejected.
- Perspective must be consistent; a new image of the same place must keep that place's perspective.
  Close-ups must not invent a new perspective; images with too little detail to hold the perspective
  are rejected.
- Real geometry beats invention: scenes built from 3D models (Blender, Sketchfab) with a consistent
  camera are preferred over images generated from nothing.
- Real historical imagery (paintings, drawings) beats AI imagery; AI generation is the last resort.
- A reference image endorses the overall look, not every element in it.
- Semi-transparent overlapping layers (e.g. clouds mixed with opacity) look wrong.

## Voice and copy
- Never American-accented voices; English is British. Each language gets a native voice.
- No marketing slop: no "Where X meets Y" taglines, no title-tagline-subheading formula.
- No em dashes in text people read; write plainly, the way a person talks. No emoji in teacher UI.
- Less is more in the interface: icons over text, no invented chrome.

## Teaching
- Clear learning goals per lesson; the quiz tests what the lesson taught.
- Roles in class games go to teams of 3-4, never to individual children.
- Age-appropriate: pupils of 12-13 meet Dante's life and a few cantos; hard words are explained.
